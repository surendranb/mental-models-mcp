# Survivorship Bias

## 1. Core Principle
Concentrating on the people or things that successfully passed a selection process while overlooking those that failed, leading to distorted, false conclusions. (Famous example: Abraham Wald and WWII bombers—reinforcing the parts of returning planes that had *no* bullet holes, because planes shot there did not return).

## 2. When NOT to Use (Failure Boundaries)
- Highly standardized, deterministic manufacturing processes with zero selection filtering.

## 3. Operational Thinking Steps
1. Identify the Unseen Graveyard: Look beyond the visible winners to discover the invisible population of failed attempts.
2. Inspect the Filtering Mechanism: What caused the failures to disappear from view?
3. Invert the Advice: Do not blindly copy the habits of successful startups or top performers; analyze whether failed entities had the exact same habits.

## 4. Diagnostic Probing Questions
- "What happened to the 99% of people or projects that followed this exact same advice and failed?"
- "Are we looking only at the returning bombers?"

## 5. Worked Example
- Context: Marketing analyzed current power users and concluded: "All our top customers use feature X, so we should force all new users through feature X during onboarding."
- Correction: Looked at churned user logs (the graveyard): discovered 80% of dropped users bounced *because* feature X was too confusing. Kept feature X optional.
